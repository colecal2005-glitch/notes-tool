A few years ago, Kamille Longstreet and her family were living in Los Angeles in an apartment where you couldn't open the oven and the silverware drawer at the same time. As time went on, they started asking a provocative question: what if we just didn't do this anymore?

The answer, it turned out, involved France. A rented farmhouse. Two and a half years of due diligence. And eventually, a small château with two towers, a private forest, a river, a chapel, and a bread oven that predates the United States. I sat down with Kamille to talk about château hunting, village life, raising kids in rural France, and what it actually feels like when the life you dreamed about becomes the one you're living.

Q. Hi Kamille, thank you for sharing a virtual cup of cafe au lait with us today. Before anything else, tell us about how you and your family ended up moving to France from Southern California.

Honestly, the short answer is: a 500-square-foot apartment and a slow-burning existential crisis.

We were four people in a one-bedroom apartment in Los Angeles. No balcony. No yard. A kitchen so small you could not open the silverware drawer and the oven at the same time (which is fine until you realize that’s a metaphor for your entire life). And yet we were somehow paying LA rent for the privilege.

But it wasn’t really the apartment. It was the culture around us. The hustle. The ambient glorification of exhaustion. Every conversation seemed to circle back to how busy everyone was, like busyness was the personality trait we were all collectively reaching for. We were tired of being tired.

We started asking a very inconvenient question: what if we just…didn’t do this anymore? What if we built something smaller and slower, somewhere else, on purpose?

France had been whispering at us for a while. So we leapt.

Two years, one rented farmhouse, and a shocking amount of bureaucracy later - we own a small château in the French countryside, and my baseline stress level has dropped to a point I didn’t actually know was available to me. It turns out the life you dream about is sometimes the one you have to actually go build. Wild concept.




Q. You gave yourselves two and a half years in a rented farmhouse before committing to buying a property in France. How did you narrow down the chateau hunting and what made the one you bought stand out among the rest?

First, a warning: château hunting is addictive and it should probably come with a disclaimer. The listings are endless. At any given moment there are hundreds for sale in France, and they are all beautiful in different ways, and you will fall in love with approximately forty of them before you close a single deal. It’s less of a house search and more of a fantasy subscription service.

We learned quickly that the price is never the whole price. A château that looks like a steal from Los Angeles (and by the way, what is a steal in Paris or LA is an actual castle in France, which still messes with my head) comes with roofs, drainage systems that predate electricity, questionable heating arrangements, and legal surprises that are very, well, French. One property we loved had a gorgeous lake view, and then the agent mentioned, almost casually, that a local farmer had the right to work the field between the château and the lake indefinitely — and that the contract transferred to his children. We owned the land. We just couldn’t use it. That was the moment we understood that the fine print in France is written in invisible ink.

After we’d seen enough to know what “livable” actually meant, we started asking less romantic questions: Can we heat this? Can we manage the grounds without becoming entirely consumed by them? Could we actually be happy here, not just Instagram-happy?

Ours is smaller than the grand ones. It came with two towers, a private forest, a river, a chapel, and an old boulangerie (a traditional outdoor bread oven). The chateau is a bit crooked, full of character, in a region we love, more ‘cozy’ than palace-like, with a storybook feel. It felt just right for our family.




Q. Your chateau came with two towers, a river, a chapel, a private forest, and some outbuildings. Where do you even begin when you start to plan renovations and making it feel like home?

The château did not wait for me to have a plan. The château presented me with a clipboard and said: choose your priorities, or be consumed.

So: triage. That is the answer. Boring, unglamorous, deeply necessary triage.

Because the temptation when you own something beautiful and old is to start with the thing you want most. The walled kitchen garden. The ornamental fountain. The outdoor dining setup for long summer evenings. And the château does not care what you want most. The château cares about order of operations.

We’re starting with safety - dead trees that could fall on someone, pool safety planning, the septic system, which is exactly as unglamorous as it sounds. Then livability: stabilizing the heat, creating zones in the house that feel functional and warm, making sure we could live here while we are also slowly restoring it. Because you cannot restore a château if the experience of living in it is making you miserable.

What surprised me most is how much we fell in love with the gardens, and decided to bump them up on the priority list. It’s quite a blank slate here, and the outdoors are so important to us. We’re eager to (literally) get our hands dirty and bring the grounds back to life.

We had the façade of the chateau cleaned early on, and I remember standing in the drive looking up at the turrets afterward thinking: there it is. Sparkling and bright. That was the first time it felt real.




Everything else is a list. A very, very long list. I’ve (mostly) made peace with the list.

Q. Several months in, you wrote about becoming locals in your small village. What surprised you most about assimilating to your new life and what characters have you met already?

When we arrived in our new town here, we were Americans in a small French village with terrible French and a château behind a gate, and I assumed we’d need to earn our way in slowly. Turns out, we did not.

We are an English-only household. My French is what I would charitably call aspirational. And yet within a few weeks, we had been invited into the homes of the dairy farmers up the hill, the retired farming couple whose kitchen walls are lined with trophies for cattle excellence (a category of award I did not previously know existed), and a woman down the road who didn’t ask us to come to her goûter - she informed us we were expected. At 2pm. Tomorrow. These are not invitations. They are calendar events delivered in person.

We showed up to that goûter to find approximately ten people seated and specifically waiting to meet us. There was galette, flan, brownies, and naturally wine (because France).

My personal favorite character so far is the gentleman who lives in the old mill that used to belong to the château. Exceptional English, exceptional sweaters. He invited us in, told us stories, handed us his business card - which had his and his ex-wife’s names on it, her name crossed out in thick black pen. Just one definitive stroke. Zero fuss. Admirably efficient.

He now texts us his favorite French music. And...face swap photos. His face on his dog. The dog’s face on him. He is completely delighted by this technology, and every photo sent has us in fits.

The thing that surprised me most, honestly, was the teenagers. The 16-year-old grandson at one of our first neighbor visits walked into a room full of strangers and gave both my children (whom he had never met) a kiss on each cheek. No awkwardness. Just warmth. In America, a 16-year-old at a family gathering is usually radiating low-grade contempt for everyone over 25. Whatever France is putting in the galette, I need the recipe!




Q. How are your kids adjusting to French life and what advice do you have for others, who may be contemplating an international move with young ones in tow?

They are thriving in ways I didn’t fully anticipate, and it has required me to unlearn a lot of things about what “thriving” is supposed to look like.

Here’s the honest version: my kids now go to a fully French school. They are English-only at home and French-only at school, which means for a while there was a language gap, and it was real, and hard, and we felt it. But something that helped: Play (which, it turns out, is its own language, and children are native speakers).

What I noticed pretty quickly was how unhurried childhood feels here. Nobody cares if your five-year-old is already reading. Nobody is benchmarking your toddler. In the US, I had absorbed, completely without realizing it, this low-level ambient panic about whether my children were ahead enough, reading early enough, ready enough. France just...doesn’t do that. The formal reading curriculum doesn’t start until age six, and by seven, French kids are fluent readers anyway - not because they were drilled, but because their foundation is built slowly and well. The children are not in a race. They’re still allowed to just be small.




France ranks third in the world for children’s wellbeing in the UNICEF report that came out last year (2025), and I actually wrote a whole article on this. When I read that statistic and then looked at my own kids - less frantic, more curious, drawing castles and dragons and having very serious ten-minute arguments about whether you’d be allowed to bring a pet dragon to Hogwarts - I thought: yes. That tracks.

My advice for anyone contemplating this: rent first, in the actual region you’re considering, before you buy anything. Live next to the life you think you want before you commit to it. We did two and a half years on the grounds of a château before we bought our own, and that time taught us more than any amount of YouTube research ever could. And then: lean into the discomfort of not knowing. The awkward French conversations, the neighbor visits where you understand 20% of what’s said and smile through the rest - that’s not the obstacle. That is the life.




Rapid Fire Round

Most absurd element of French bureaucracy you’ve encountered so far? I would probably have to say the inconsistency. There’s no rulebook you can actually look up, because every office, every town, every département does things differently, and sometimes it just comes down to whoever is behind the desk that day and what mood they woke up in. You can’t Google your way out of it. You just have to show up, be patient, let go, and persist. Which, honestly, is also just the lesson France keeps teaching me about nearly everything.

A new French habit you’ve fully adopted? Guilt-free rest. I was not raised for this. I had to be broken down and rebuilt. But the French treat rest like a legitimate activity - not laziness, not procrastination, just rest - and I have fully converted. Also: sacred Sundays. Everything is closed, no one is available, and the whole country collectively agrees to just be home. That’s not an inconvenience, but rather, a policy I want to export!




One thing you miss from California? It’s hard to pick just one! But, I would have to say Mexican food. I mean, I came from Southern California! This is a wound that does not heal. I miss other things too (same-day shipping, stores open past 7pm, Target, everything being in English), but Mexican food is the one I think about when I’m standing in a French supermarket staring at the “international foods” aisle, wondering why there’s a whole section for British biscuits but nothing I can make into a taco.

The most surprising thing you found in your castle (so far)? Two categories: outdoor and indoor, because they’re pretty much tied and I can’t choose!

Outdoor: In early April, we walked into our private forest and found an absolute carpet of bluebells. Wall to wall, as far as you could see, like something out of a fairy tale. Not mentioned in the listing, not in the brochure, not a word from the realtor or the previous owners. We had no idea. We just wandered in one morning and stood there with our mouths open. That moment was free, and it might be the most beautiful thing about the grounds.




Indoor: The boulangerie - an old stone bread oven built into one of the small outbuildings, blackened from centuries of actual use, where the family would have baked bread going back hundreds of years. I stood in front of it thinking: someone has been doing this in this exact spot since before America was a country.




And then there’s the one we haven’t found yet. There’s a historical record of a hidden underground tunnel that runs from the château to a neighboring town. It’s just...a thing about our house, apparently. We will find it eventually (probably!).

Finish this sentence: “I knew we’d made the right decision when ___.”

...we hit the one-year mark and I realized I was just living - not performing, not auditing, not white-knuckling through it. Less stress, more time with the kids, more time outside, more space in every sense of the word. The first year is a mix of honeymoon and survival mode. But when it ended and I looked around at our actual life? It was exactly what we came for.
