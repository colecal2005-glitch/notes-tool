The Wilderness Act is the most ambitious land protection law in American history. It built a system that now covers 803 places and roughly 111.7 million acres, an area larger than California. It says, in writing, that some places are not for sale.

This is the latest in our series on the laws that protect America’s public lands. We’ve covered NEPA, the Endangered Species Act, the Congressional Review Act, and FLPMA. Today we’re covering the law that draws the brightest line.

Most Americans have never read a word of the Wilderness Act. They don’t know who wrote it. They don’t know who passed it. They don’t know that an Idaho senator dying of cancer kept fighting for it past every reasonable election calculation, or that the man who drafted it died four months before it became law and never saw it signed.

They should. Because the law is under attack, and the people attacking it are counting on you not knowing what you stand to lose.

Here’s what the Wilderness Act is, how it works, the people who fought for it, and what’s happening to it right now.

What It Is
The Wilderness Act of 1964 created the National Wilderness Preservation System. It established a category of federal land protection stronger than any other in American law.

President Lyndon Johnson signed it on September 3, 1964, after eight years of fighting and 66 separate drafts. The bill protected 9.1 million acres in 13 states from the start. Today, after 60 years of additions, the system covers 803 separate wilderness areas across 44 states and Puerto Rico.

The law’s most quoted line is also its core idea. Wilderness is land where the earth and its community of life are untrammeled by man, where man himself is a visitor who does not remain.

That sentence does real legal work. It’s why you can’t drive a truck into a wilderness area, why you can’t run a chainsaw, why you can’t open a mine, why you can’t build a road. The law forbids permanent roads, motor vehicles, motorboats, mechanical transport, landing aircraft, structures, and installations. Bicycles are out. Drones are out. Helicopter landings are out. Logging is out. New mining claims are out. Commercial enterprise is out, with narrow exceptions written into the law itself.

Wilderness sits inside the federal land you already own. It overlays national parks, national forests, national wildlife refuges, and Bureau of Land Management lands. The Forest Service manages the most wilderness areas at 448 units. The Park Service manages the most acres at roughly 43.9 million. Fish and Wildlife and BLM manage the rest.

Only Congress can create a wilderness area. Only Congress can take one away. That last part matters more than any other detail in this piece.

How It Works
The mechanics are simple by design. Howard Zahniser, the Wilderness Society’s executive director, wrote them that way on purpose.

A wilderness area starts as a proposal. Congress passes a bill. The president signs it. The land becomes wilderness. From that day forward, the rules apply.

The agencies that manage wilderness, the Forest Service, Park Service, Fish and Wildlife, and BLM, have to manage it consistent with the law. They can’t decide to allow a road. They can’t decide to allow a mine. They can’t decide a logging contract is fine because the timber is dying. The law removed that discretion from them on purpose, because the Forest Service had spent decades shrinking its own administratively designated wilderness areas every time industry came knocking.

That’s the genius of the law. Zahniser had watched the Forest Service create primitive areas in the 1930s and then carve them up whenever a road or a mine seemed convenient. He decided the only fix was to take the decision away from the agency and put it in statute. Once Congress designated a place as wilderness, no agency could undesignate it. No president could undesignate it. Only Congress could, and Congress would have to do it in writing, in public, with a recorded vote.

That structure is why the system has grown almost every year since 1964 and why it has only rarely shrunk.

The People Who Fought For It
Howard Zahniser drafted the bill in 1956. He spent the next eight years lobbying Congress for it. He revised it 66 times and steered it through 18 hearings. He died of a heart attack in May 1964, four months before Johnson signed it into law. He never saw the bill pass.

Aldo Leopold convinced the Forest Service to administratively protect the Gila Wilderness in New Mexico in 1924, the first such designation anywhere in the world. The idea that wilderness could be preserved as wilderness, rather than developed into something useful, started there.

Bob Marshall founded the Wilderness Society in 1935. Olaus and Mardy Murie kept it going through the war years. Sigurd Olson testified before Congress repeatedly and helped Zahniser draft the bill itself. There were hundreds of others.

But the man who got it across the finish line was Frank Church.

Frank Church was a Democrat from Idaho who served in the Senate from 1957 to 1981. He was 32 when he got there, the fifth youngest senator in U.S. history at the time. He’d been diagnosed with testicular cancer at 24 and given a few months to live. He survived, finished law school, won an upset election to the Senate, and decided that if he was going to be there, he was going to do something with the time.

When Senator Clinton Anderson of New Mexico, the bill’s lead sponsor, had a gallbladder attack on the eve of the 1961 Senate floor debate, he asked Church to take over. Church didn’t want to. His staff warned him the timber industry, the mining industry, the cattlemen, and the chambers of commerce in his own state would come for him. His father-in-law called him and told him he was out of his mind, that if he attached his name to the Wilderness Act he was toast.

Church did it anyway.

He floor-managed the bill through the Senate, which passed it 78-8 in September 1961. The House refused to act. Anderson re-introduced it in 1963. The Senate passed it again 73-12. The House finally passed it 374-1 in 1964. Johnson signed it that September.

Church won re-election in 1962 against the unified opposition of every major resource industry in Idaho. He won again in 1968. He won again in 1974. He became the only Idaho Democrat ever popularly elected to more than one term in the Senate.

He wasn’t done. In 1968 he sponsored the Wild and Scenic Rivers Act. In 1980, his last year in office, he passed the Central Idaho Wilderness Act and protected 2.4 million acres of central Idaho as the River of No Return Wilderness. It is still the largest contiguous wilderness in the lower 48. Church lost his Senate seat that November in the Reagan landslide. He was hospitalized for a pancreatic tumor on January 12, 1984.

In late February 1984, Republican Senator Jim McClure of Idaho introduced a bill to add Church’s name to the wilderness he’d created. Reagan signed it on March 14. Frank Church died on April 7, less than four weeks later, at age 59. When his friend Doug Scott visited him at his bedside, Church didn’t want to talk about Vietnam or the Church Committee or the CIA. He wanted to talk about the wilderness.

That’s the law we’re talking about. Written by a man who didn’t live to see it pass. Carried across the line by a man who took every political hit it cost him and asked for more. Built one piece at a time by people who understood the country would not give them another chance.

What’s In The System
Some of the wilderness areas you’ve heard of, even if you didn’t know they were wilderness.

The Boundary Waters Canoe Area Wilderness in Minnesota. The Bob Marshall in Montana. The Selway-Bitterroot. The Frank Church-River of No Return. The Eagle Cap, in the Wallowas of Oregon, where the Nez Perce were forced from their homeland. The Pasayten in Washington. The Three Sisters and the Mount Hood Wilderness. The Death Valley Wilderness, the largest named wilderness in the lower 48. The Wrangell-Saint Elias Wilderness in Alaska, more than 9 million acres on its own and the largest single wilderness area in the United States. The Gila in New Mexico, the original.

Wilderness is the headwaters of most of the major rivers in the West. It’s the last secure habitat for grizzly bears, wolverines, lynx, fisher, sage grouse. It’s where the Pacific Crest Trail spends much of its 2,650 miles. It’s where the Continental Divide Trail runs. It’s why your tap water is clean if you live in a Western city.

It’s also small. Wilderness covers about 4.5% of the United States. Strip out Alaska and it’s 2.7% of the contiguous 48. The thing the law protects is, in landscape terms, the last little bit. There isn’t more of it being made.

What’s Happening To It Right Now
The Wilderness Act is harder to attack than NEPA or FLPMA because the protections are written into statute and only Congress can revoke them. So the current attack works around the edges instead.

The Trump administration is rescinding the Roadless Rule, which protected 58.5 million acres of inventoried roadless areas in national forests. Roadless areas are not wilderness. They are the buffer around wilderness. They are the lands that should have been wilderness but never quite made it through Congress. They are lands that still could be wilderness. Strip the Roadless Rule and you open the doors to wilderness from the outside.

The administration is rolling back protections for the Arctic National Wildlife Refuge, which contains millions of acres of designated wilderness, by ordering lease sales on the Coastal Plain. This industrial activity on the coastal plain, though technically outside the wilderness would have direct negative impacts on the wilderness within the refuge. Earthjustice and partner groups have sued, citing the Wilderness Act among other laws.

It’s revoked the mineral withdrawal protecting the Boundary Waters Canoe Area Wilderness watershed from sulfide-ore copper-nickel mining, after a 50-49 Senate vote on April 16 we’ll spare you the recap of. The mine itself would sit outside the wilderness. The pollution it produces would flow directly into it.

It’s greenlighted gold exploration on Conglomerate Mesa, a former Wilderness Study Area in the California desert that retains outstanding wilderness values. Wilderness Study Areas are the bench. They’re the lands that have been identified as deserving wilderness protection but haven’t been designated yet. Once you drill 76 holes into one, the wilderness values are gone.

It’s dismantled the Forest Service, the agency that manages the most wilderness in the country. It’s gutted NEPA and is currently pushing the Fix Our Forests Act through the Senate. Both make it dramatically easier to log, mine, and drill in landscapes that border wilderness, with shorter review periods, fewer studies, and stripped-down judicial review.

None of this is a frontal assault on the Wilderness Act. The administration knows the political cost of trying to actually delist a wilderness area. So instead it’s hollowing out everything around the wilderness, knowing that wilderness without its surrounding watershed, its surrounding migration corridors, its surrounding roadless backcountry, is a museum piece. A line on a map.

That is the strategy. Leave the wilderness area technically protected. Destroy everything that makes the wilderness work.

Why It Matters
Wilderness is the most successful land protection idea this country has ever produced. Countries from Australia to Canada to Finland to South Africa have built their own wilderness laws on the American model.

Frank Church understood the politics of wilderness in a way most people don’t. The argument against wilderness, then and now, was that it was elitist. That only rich East Coast hikers with pack animals would ever use it. Church spent his career batting that down. He pointed out that on his trips into the Sawtooth, he was the only one with a horse. Everyone else had a backpack.

Wilderness is the most democratic kind of public land in the country. There’s no entry fee. There’s no permit system in most areas. You don’t need a guide. You don’t need money. You need legs and time and the willingness to be uncomfortable.

It’s also the public land that does the most for everyone, including the people who never go. Wilderness areas sequester carbon at a rate roughly equivalent to all other federal lands combined. They produce a disproportionate share of the country’s drinking water. They provide critical habitat for species that have nowhere else left. They draw migrants to nearby rural counties, which is why Western counties with wilderness nearby grew their in-migration by an average of 8% from 1980 to 2010 while comparable counties without wilderness saw zero growth.

If you’ve ever drunk water from a tap in Boise, Salt Lake City, Denver, Portland, Seattle, or Los Angeles, you’ve drunk water that came from wilderness. If you’ve ever seen a grizzly bear in the lower 48, it’s because there was wilderness for it to live in. If you’ve ever stood somewhere where you couldn’t see a road or a powerline, the only reason that place still exists is that someone put it on paper sixty years ago and refused to back down.

Frank Church died believing his greatest accomplishment wasn’t the Church Committee or stopping the Vietnam War or the Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Act. It was the wilderness. He told his friends that and he meant it.

What You Can Do
The Wilderness Act survives because Congress is the only body that can undo it. That’s the moat. That’s the structural feature that has kept it intact through 12 administrations.

Right now, the most effective thing you can do is protect the surrounding system. Stopping the Roadless Rule rescission. Stopping the Fix Our Forests Act. Stopping the Arctic Refuge lease sales. Stopping the next monument revocation. Each of those fights is a fight to keep wilderness functional.
